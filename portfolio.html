case study

A concept bookshop,
and the reasoning under it

Between the Veil is a self-directed concept by Sagewave Web Design — an imaginary bookshop, built to show authors and bookish businesses what their own site could be instead of describing it to them in a proposal.

The brief I set myself

Romantasy readers are one of the most visually literate audiences online — they have strong, specific taste and they can tell instantly when something was made carelessly. I wanted a piece aimed squarely at that eye, rather than another neutral agency landing page.

The second goal was practical. Indie and debut authors regularly have no website at all, and no clear idea what one should cost or contain — which makes them hard to sell to in the abstract. A finished, specific, slightly absurd example answers the question faster than any pitch deck.

So everything here is a working demonstration rather than a mockup: the entrance animates, the shelves filter, the author feature is laid out as though a real person were in it. The shop is invented. The build is not.

The entrance, in one line:
knock → both halves swing on their own hinge → candlelight blooms through the seam → step through.

The door

A single photograph of a gothic door, sliced down its centre seam into two files. Each half is an absolutely positioned element with its own transform-origin — left edge for the left half, right edge for the right — so they rotate outward on real hinges rather than sliding apart.

The parent carries perspective: 1400px, which is what makes the swing read as depth instead of a squash. Behind both halves sits a radial gradient that widens and brightens as they open, so light appears to spill out of the gap.

On wide screens the door caps at 620px and centres rather than stretching. A door stretched to fill a 1920px monitor stops looking like a door.

Two halves · two hinges · one glow
—
swap in any straight-on door photo, split it down the middle at the same two filenames, and the animation just works.

Craft notes

Palette & type

A faerie-twilight ground with three accents — moss, rose and candlelight gold — each assigned a job rather than sprinkled. Cinzel carries the carved, inscribed feeling; Quicksand keeps the body copy warm enough that the gothic never tips into cold.

Reduced motion

The entrance is entirely motion. Under prefers-reduced-motion the door still opens and you still get through, but the swing, the mote drift and the veil menu all resolve instantly instead of animating. The particle system doesn’t even instantiate.

Never trapped outside

An animated gate is a genuine accessibility risk, so there’s a keyboard-reachable skip on the threshold, and entry is remembered per session — navigating between pages never puts the door back in your way.

No build step

Hand-written HTML, one stylesheet, three small scripts, zero dependencies. It deploys by copying a folder. For a site this size a toolchain would have been cost without benefit.

Mobile-first, genuinely

Built at phone width first, then widened through three breakpoints. Grids go 1 → 2 → 3 columns; the nav switches from a drawn-across veil to a horizontal bar at 1024px.

Content as data

The shelves render from a single array rather than a pile of hand-written cards, so renaming a mood or adding a title is a one-line change instead of a find-and-replace across the markup.

Text-only book cards

A design decision made for a legal reason. Book titles and author names aren’t copyrightable, so real recommendations are safe to list — but cover art is, and reproducing it without a license isn’t. Losing the covers meant the typography had to carry the grid, which turned out better anyway.

Honest about being a concept

The shop is invented and says so, in the footer of every page and in the link previews. An earlier draft invited real authors to submit to a spotlight that would never run — a fine-looking call to action, and a promise the site couldn’t keep.
